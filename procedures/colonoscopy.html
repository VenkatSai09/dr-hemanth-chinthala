Colonoscopy

A colonoscopy examines the whole large bowel, the colon and rectum, with a flexible camera. It finds the cause of symptoms such as bleeding or a change in bowel habit, and polyps can be removed during the same test.

When it is advised

  • Blood in the stool.
  • A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks.
  • Iron-deficiency anaemia or weight loss without a clear reason.
  • Long-standing diarrhoea.
  • Diagnosing and monitoring Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.
  • Follow-up after polyps have been removed.
  • Screening for bowel cancer.

Most bowel cancers start as small growths called polyps. Removing them during a colonoscopy stops them turning into cancer.

Preparing your bowel

The bowel must be completely empty so nothing is missed. You will get written instructions; usually:

  • A low-fibre diet for a day or two before.
  • A laxative drink the evening before and, often, early on the morning of the test. Stay near a toilet.
  • Only clear fluids after the laxative.
  • Iron tablets are often stopped a few days before. Discuss blood thinners and diabetes medicines with the team.

How it is done

You are given sedation. The test usually takes 20 to 45 minutes. Air or carbon dioxide gently opens the bowel so the lining can be seen. Biopsies and polyp removal are painless.

After the test

You may feel bloated for a few hours. Do not drive for the rest of the day. Dr Chinthala will tell you what he found; biopsy results take a few working days.

Risks

Colonoscopy is safe. There is a small risk of bleeding after a polyp is removed, and, rarely, of a tear in the bowel wall.

Common questions

Is the preparation difficult?

Drinking the laxative is the least pleasant part, but it is essential. Following the instructions closely means you are less likely to need the test repeated.

Will it hurt?

With sedation most people are comfortable and remember little. You may feel some cramping or bloating, which passes quickly.

When should I have a screening colonoscopy?

Screening is usually considered from about 45 to 50, and earlier if a close relative had bowel cancer or polyps. Ask Dr Chinthala whether it is right for you.
