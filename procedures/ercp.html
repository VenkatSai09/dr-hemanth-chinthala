ERCP: treating the bile and pancreatic ducts without surgery

ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) uses a flexible scope passed through the mouth to reach the opening of the bile and pancreatic ducts in the duodenum. Through it, blockages can be cleared, stones removed and stents placed, usually without any cut.

When ERCP is needed

  • Stones in the bile duct, which often slip down from the gallbladder and can cause pain, jaundice or infection.
  • Jaundice from a blocked duct, when the yellowing of the eyes and skin comes from a narrowing or a growth.
  • Narrowing of the ducts (strictures), which may need a stent to keep bile flowing.
  • Bile leaks after gallbladder surgery.
  • Some problems of the pancreatic duct, such as stones or narrowing in chronic pancreatitis.

ERCP is now used mainly to treat. If the only question is what is causing the problem, an MRCP scan or endoscopic ultrasound can usually answer it without the risks of ERCP.

How it is done

You are given sedation or anaesthesia, so you are comfortable and usually do not remember the procedure. The scope is passed through the mouth to the duodenum. A thin tube is guided into the bile or pancreatic duct, and dye is injected so the ducts show on X-ray.

A small cut in the duct opening (a sphincterotomy) widens it. Stones are then pulled out with a balloon or basket, and a stent, a small tube, can be left across a narrowing. ERCP usually takes 30 to 90 minutes.

Before the procedure

  • Nothing to eat for about six hours before, unless you are told otherwise.
  • Tell the team about blood thinners, diabetes medicines and any allergy, especially to contrast dye or iodine.
  • You will need recent blood tests, and someone to take you home.

Risks

In experienced hands ERCP is safe, but it is more involved than a routine endoscopy. The most common complication is pancreatitis, an inflammation of the pancreas, which happens in a small number of patients and usually settles with treatment. Less common are bleeding, infection and a tear in the gut wall. Dr Chinthala will explain which risks apply to you and how they are kept low.

After ERCP

You are watched for a few hours. Many people go home the same day or the next morning. A sore throat and some bloating are common. Contact the hospital at once if you have severe belly pain, fever, repeated vomiting or black stools.

Common questions

Is ERCP painful?

You are given sedation or anaesthesia, so most people feel nothing during the procedure. Afterwards you may have a sore throat or mild bloating for a day.

How long will I stay in hospital?

Often only a few hours, or one night. You may stay longer if ERCP was done for an infection or if a complication needs watching.

Will I need my gallbladder removed?

If the stones came from the gallbladder, removing it is often advised so they do not come back. Dr Chinthala and the surgeon will advise you on the timing.

What is a stent, and is it permanent?

A stent is a small plastic or metal tube that keeps a duct open. Plastic stents are usually changed or removed within a few months. Some metal stents are left in place.
